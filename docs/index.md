## 5. Finishing, Joining, and Final Inspection

Preserved shop record for santourian.com. The former plant is not quoting from this domain. The sections above describe precision sheet metal fabrication, stainless steel work, custom fabrication, and precision stamping, and this closing note stays inside that process language to cover the end of the line, where a part stops being work in process and becomes a delivered product. Joining is the first of these final disciplines. Weldments, rivets, PEM hardware, and fasteners each carry their own rules: weld heat distorts, rivets need access, self-clinching hardware needs the right panel thickness and hole size. A shop that plans joining at the design stage ships straighter parts than one that treats it as an afterthought. Finishing follows. Deburring and edge conditioning make parts safe to handle and ready to coat. Powder coat, plating, passivation for stainless, and paint each answer a different service environment, and the finish specification belongs on the print, not in someone's memory. Masking, surface preparation, and cure control decide whether the finish survives the field. Inspection closes the record. Dimensional checks against the drawing, hole location verification, hardware pull checks where required, and a final look at cosmetics before packing are what separate a precision shop from a job shop that happens to own the same machines. Packing itself belongs in the sequence, because a precision part damaged in transit is a precision part wasted. Read as a whole, the archive preserves a complete working chain: blanking and stamping at the front, forming and joining in the middle, finishing and inspection at the end, with short-run discipline holding the tolerances together when volume cannot. That complete chain is the shop record this index keeps. The short-run frame matters here as well, because finishing and inspection are where small orders most often go wrong: coating schedules, cure ovens, and inspection time do not shrink just because the batch is small, and a shop that understands that prices and schedules its short runs accordingly. For anyone reviewing a fabricated assembly before acceptance, the archive's end-of-line order is the right one to copy: verify joining and hardware first, then finish and cosmetics, then the dimensional record, and only then release the part to packing.
